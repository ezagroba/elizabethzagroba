At the Friends of Good Software last weekend, Christian Pfeiffer hosted a session about how to be a "good" staff engineer / lead engineer. Specifically, he wanted to us share what behaviors we wanted to see in such a person. He'd read the Tanya Reilly book _The Staff Engineer's Path_ but wanted to include people in the session who may not have read the book. 

Christian did something I haven't seen at FroGS before, and will be stealing to use in future sessions myself: he began the session by asking us to take a few quiet minutes to write our ideas on stickies. He would choose which ideas he wanted to dig into more. Later in the open space, someone asked how our session ended up with so many [notes](https://elizabethzagroba.com/posts/2026/02_13_noted/). It was because Christian created an environment where many note-takers were not only desired, or requested, but required for the session to proceed. 

This facilitation technique, and the framing of the question to include those who hadn't read the book, both came up as examples of the kind of behavior we'd want to see from a staff engineer. It lines up with Jerry Weinberg's definition of technical leadership: creating an environment where everyone can contribute to solving the problem. (The resulting recommendation was one of many books recommended in this session. I included links at the bottom.)

The lucky participants who joined FroGS last weekend have access to the full, rich notes from the whole session. I just want to pull out a few behaviors we identified in good staff engineers because they had a theme in common: fish. 

### 1. Teach someone to fish

The common English phrasing would be:

> Give a man a fish, and you feed him for a day. Teach a man To fish, and you feed him for a lifetime.

But it's 2026, so we're not issuing fish exclusively to men these days. 

Rather than being the expert, the bottleneck, the firefighter, the solver of problems, [the provider of fish](https://elizabethzagroba.com/posts/2021/give_them_the_fish_then_teach_them_to_fish/), the goal of a staff engineer should be to teach someone to fish. Staff engineers should be growing the skills in people around them, raising the "next generation" of software professionals.

### 2. Stop slapping me in the face with the fish

Slapping someone in the face with the fish goes farther than giving someone a fish -- it requires that they accept it, whether they like it or not. Staff engineers should not be forcing solution directions upon their fellow engineers. They should [facilitate the conversations](https://elizabethzagroba.com/posts/2023/04_10_anarchist_software_architecture/) that allow the best ideas to emerge. 

Thanks to Joep Schuurkes for tracking down the Monty Python clip of the [fish slapping dance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sq7pCTQCiDc). 

### 3. Put the fish on the table before it rots

Lisi Hocke brought this metaphor. Putting the fish on the table before it rots is akin to pointing out the elephant in the room. It's putting a name to the unspoken tension in a conversation. It's clearing the air so the group can figure out how to move on. Rather than letting a problem become so rotten that it can't be easily resolved, staff engineers learn to put the fish on the table soon enough.
